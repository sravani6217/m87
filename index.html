What is M87*?

M87* is the supermassive black hole located near the centre of the giant elliptical galaxy Messier 87. Its enormous mass produces extreme spacetime curvature. Matter orbiting in its surroundings can become extremely hot and emit radiation.

The bright ring in the famous EHT image is radiation from hot material around the black hole, shaped by gravitational lensing. The dark central region is associated with the black-hole shadow.

  • How time and light frequency are affected by gravity.

Important regions

The boundary beyond which no signal or light can escape to distant observers.

2. Photon Sphere

GENERAL RELATIVITY / 03

Schwarzschild Theory

Understanding the gravitational radius of an ideal non-rotating black hole.

Schwarzschild Radius rs = 2GM / c²

G = gravitational constant · M = mass · c = speed of light

What does the equation mean?

Karl Schwarzschild found an exact solution to Einstein's field equations for the vacuum outside a spherical, non-rotating mass. The Schwarzschild radius identifies the event-horizon radius in this idealised model.

If an object's mass is compressed within this radius, the Schwarzschild solution describes a black-hole event horizon.

Change the mass

Calculated Schwarzschild radius 19.2 billion km

This is an ideal Schwarzschild estimate. M87* may rotate, so a realistic rotating black hole requires the Kerr solution.

Why use this theory?

It provides a simple way to estimate the scale of a black-hole horizon from its mass. It also introduces the gravitational length scale used to describe photon orbits, light bending and relativistic effects.

Accretion Disc

How does an accretion disc work?

Gas and plasma can spiral towards a black hole while conserving angular momentum. Interactions within the disc transport angular momentum outward, allowing some material to move inward.

The material heats up and emits electromagnetic radiation. The black hole itself is not shining; much of the observed emission comes from its surrounding environment.

Relativistic Jet

M87 is famous for its enormous jet of energetic particles.

How are jets produced?

Strong magnetic fields in the rotating black-hole environment and surrounding accretion flow can help launch and collimate relativistic outflows.

The jet does not emerge from inside the event horizon. It is launched from the region outside the horizon, where magnetic fields and plasma interact.

EINSTEIN'S GENERAL RELATIVITY / 06

Spacetime Curvature

Mass and energy influence the geometry of spacetime.

Illustrative curved-grid representation.

What is spacetime?

General Relativity describes gravity not simply as a pulling force, but as the effect of curved spacetime. Matter and energy influence spacetime geometry, and freely falling objects follow paths called geodesics.

Near M87*, the curvature is extreme. This affects the motion of matter and the paths followed by light.

Einstein Field Equations Gμν + Λgμν = 8πG Tμν / c⁴

Geometry of spacetime is related to matter and energy.

GRAVITATIONAL LENSING / 07

How Light Travels Near M87*

Light follows curved paths through curved spacetime.

Simplified light-ray illustration, not a numerical geodesic solution.

Model interpretation Strong bending region

What happens to light?

Light travels along null geodesics in spacetime. A ray passing near a black hole can be deflected by gravity. The closer it passes, the stronger the deflection generally becomes.

In the Schwarzschild model, the photon sphere is at 1.5 Rs. The critical impact parameter for a ray arriving from far away is approximately 2.598 Rs. These are different quantities.

A ray that crosses the event horizon cannot escape back to distant space.

What is an orbit?

An orbit is a trajectory followed under gravity. In General Relativity, freely falling objects follow timelike geodesics. Their possible orbits depend on mass, angular momentum and spacetime geometry.

For an ideal Schwarzschild black hole, the innermost stable circular orbit for a test particle is at 6 Rs. This is a reference result, not a universal boundary for rotating black holes.

RELATIVISTIC EFFECTS / 09

Time Dilation & Gravitational Redshift

Gravity affects clock rates and the frequency of light.

Clock-rate ratio (static Schwarzschild observer) 0.894

Frequency ratio received far away 0.894

Illustrative gravitational redshift for a stationary emitter and distant stationary observer in Schwarzschild spacetime.

Gravitational time dilation

In a gravitational field, clocks at different gravitational potentials can accumulate different amounts of proper time. In the Schwarzschild solution, a stationary clock outside the horizon has a rate factor of √(1 − Rs/r) relative to a distant clock.

Gravitational redshift

Light travelling outward from a gravitational field loses frequency as measured by a distant stationary observer. Its wavelength is increased.

BLACK HOLE THERMODYNAMICS / 10

Temperature & Entropy

Black holes have thermodynamic properties in quantum theory.

Hawking Temperature — Schwarzschild black hole T = ℏc³ / (8πGMkB)

Temperature is inversely proportional to black-hole mass.

Approximate Hawking temperature 9.5 × 10⁻¹⁸ K

Hawking radiation

Quantum field theory in curved spacetime predicts that black holes have a temperature and can emit Hawking radiation. For astrophysical black holes, this temperature is extraordinarily small.

Black-hole entropy

Bekenstein and Hawking showed that black-hole entropy is proportional to the area of the event horizon, rather than the volume inside it.

Bekenstein–Hawking Entropy S = kB A c³ / (4Gℏ)

A = event-horizon area.

These are theoretical results. This educational module calculates ideal Schwarzschild values, not measured temperature or entropy of M87*.

RESEARCH CONTEXT / 11

Why M87* Matters Today

Black holes are natural laboratories for extreme physics.

1. Testing General Relativity

The observed black-hole environment allows scientists to test predictions of gravity in an extreme regime.

2. Understanding galaxy evolution

Powerful jets and energy released by accretion can influence gas around galaxies and affect star formation.

3. Studying high-energy astrophysics

M87* provides an opportunity to investigate plasma, magnetic fields, radiation and relativistic particle flows.

4. Improving imaging techniques

The Event Horizon Telescope combines radio observatories across Earth using very-long-baseline interferometry. This demonstrates the importance of advanced imaging, signal processing and international scientific collaboration.

Research objective of this project

To explore how the mass and extreme gravitational environment of M87* influence the event horizon, accretion processes, light propagation and relativistic phenomena through interactive educational models.
